# 3. Content integrity gate

A reference wiki of a few hundred entries had been bulk generated by a previous
process. Every page returned 200. Every page had a title, a body, structured
data and an image. Nothing in any structural check was failing.

Roughly **one in three entries had a body about a different subject than its
title**.

## Why nothing caught it

Every automated check in place was structural. Does the page load, does it have
a title, is the schema valid, is the body long enough. An entry titled
"Ethereum" containing a well-written article about Solana passes all of those
perfectly. It is fluent, it is the right length, it is on-topic for the site. It
is simply the wrong article.

Spot checks did not catch it either, because a reviewer opening three entries
has a two-in-three chance of seeing correct ones, and the incorrect ones read
fine in isolation.

## The signal

An entry should name its own subject, early, and more often than any competing
subject drawn from the same vocabulary. That is the whole check.

```
mismatch  the subject never appears in its own body
mismatch  a rival subject appears more often than the subject itself
review    the subject first appears past the opening
review    a rival subject is nearly as prominent
review    the body is a stub
```

No model call, no embeddings. It runs over hundreds of entries in about a
second, it is deterministic, and it can therefore sit in CI. Working version
with tests: [`examples/content-integrity`](../examples/content-integrity).

Two implementation details that matter more than they look:

**Word boundaries.** A naive substring search finds "ETH" inside "whether" and
inside "Ethereum", which makes every entry look fine. Boundary-anchored matching
is the difference between a working check and a useless one.

**Position, not just count.** A correct entry names its subject in the opening
sentence. One mention in a "see also" at the bottom is a different situation,
and worth flagging separately rather than passing.

## The quarantine contract

The interesting part was not finding the defect. It was shipping the fix without
breaking the site, because those URLs were live and linked.

Deleting the bad entries would have produced hundreds of 404s. Leaving them
would have kept wrong information indexed. So entries that fail the check:

- **stay published**, so every URL still resolves
- carry a `NEEDS_REWRITE` flag
- are set to `noindex`
- are **excluded from structured data and from the sitemap** until they pass

That turns a blocking cleanup into a background one. The site is honest with
search engines immediately, nothing 404s, and entries rejoin the index one at a
time as they are rewritten. The audit re-runs after every new batch, so the
count only goes down.

## What it got wrong on a different content shape

The first version took the subject straight from the title, which is correct for
a reference entry called "Ethereum" and wrong for an article called "Why planned
maintenance costs less than reactive repair". Matching a whole sentence against
a body finds nothing, so every healthy article was flagged.

Entries can now declare a `subject` while the title stays for display. Guessing
the noun phrase out of a title is the kind of cleverness that fails silently, so
it is asked for instead.

Running it over a fresh set of twenty service and blog entries then produced
three useful results in a row:

- Most entries failed at first because the copy **never named its own topic**.
  That is not a gate problem. Vague copy that will not commit to naming its
  subject is both what the check rejects and what a reader recognises as
  generated. The fix went into the assembler, which now guarantees the subject
  appears in the opening sentence.
- Two entries still failed, and both were genuine: one blog post mentioned
  another post's subject as often as its own, and a second was mostly about a
  topic that already had its own page. That is topical cannibalisation, found
  automatically, before publish.
- The copy was tightened and all twenty passed.

A check that only ever confirms what you already believe is not doing anything.
This one twice told me my content was wrong, and it was right both times.

## The general lesson

Structural validation tells you a page is well formed. It cannot tell you the
page is about what it says it is about. For any pipeline producing generated
content at volume, a cheap semantic consistency check is worth more than a
thorough structural one, and it has to be a **gate** rather than a report. A
report is something somebody reads later. A non-zero exit code is something that
stops a publish.
